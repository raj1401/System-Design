CAP Theorem: Consistency, Availability, and Partition Tolerance

Why distributed data stores cannot promise linearizability and uninterrupted responses when the network splits — and how modern systems actually navigate that tradeoff.

September 12, 2026 · 5 min read

The Concept, In Short

The CAP theorem (also called Brewer's theorem) states that a networked shared-data system cannot simultaneously guarantee consistency, availability, and partition tolerance. Eric Brewer presented the idea as a conjecture at PODC 2000; Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch of MIT proved it formally in 2002, establishing it as a theorem rather than folk wisdom.

In Gilbert and Lynch's formulation, consistency means atomic (linearizable) behavior: every read returns the most recent completed write (or an error). Availability means every request to a non-failing node eventually gets a response. Partition tolerance means the system keeps operating even when arbitrarily many messages between nodes are lost. During a network split, you must either refuse some requests (lose availability) or risk returning stale data (lose consistency). There is no third option that preserves both.

The key correction: the popular “pick any two of three” slogan is misleading. Brewer later clarified that partitions are not a design choice you can simply refuse — networks fail, fibers get cut, BGP misconfigures. The real decision is: when a partition happens, do you favor consistency or availability? Outside of partitions, you can (and should) aim for both C and A.

How the Impossibility Actually Works

Imagine two nodes, G1 and G2, that can no longer exchange messages. A client writes a new value to G1; by availability the write must complete. Later a client reads from G2; by availability G2 must answer. But G2 never saw the write, so if it returns the old value it violates linearizability, and if it refuses to answer it violates availability. That constructive argument is the heart of Gilbert and Lynch's proof in both the asynchronous and partially synchronous models.

What each letter really means

C — Consistency. CAP's C is single-copy linearizability, not the “C” in ACID. ACID consistency is about preserving database invariants (unique keys, foreign keys); CAP consistency is about every node agreeing on the latest value of a single object. Confusing the two letters is a common source of design mistakes.

A — Availability. CAP availability is stronger than everyday “high availability” SLAs: every non-failed node that receives a request must respond, even if it sits alone on the wrong side of a partition. Timeouts that abort an operation to protect consistency are, under CAP, a deliberate sacrifice of A.

P — Partition tolerance. In practice you cannot “choose CA and forfeit P.” If a partition occurs, a CA-oriented design must still fall back to CP or AP behavior. Choosing CA only makes sense when the probability of partitions is negligible compared with other failures (for example, a carefully engineered single datacenter).

Partition Forces the C vs A Choice

Beyond “2 of 3”: Managing Partitions

Brewer's 2012 retrospective argued that CAP only forbids a tiny corner of the design space: perfect consistency and perfect availability during a partition. Everywhere else there is room to optimize. A practical partition strategy has three steps: detect the partition (usually via timeouts), enter an explicit partition mode that may limit risky operations, and run recovery that restores consistency and compensates for mistakes once communication returns.

Partitions and latency are deeply related. Operationally, a partition is a time bound on communication: retry forever and you have chosen C over A; proceed without agreement and you risk inconsistency. Systems with tighter latency SLOs enter “partition mode” more often — sometimes when the network is merely slow.

CP-leaning systems

  • Prefer linearizability; minority partitions refuse writes
  • Use consensus (Raft, Paxos) or quorums
  • Examples: etcd, ZooKeeper, Spanner, HBase
  • Right for money movement, locks, config

AP-leaning systems

  • Keep answering on both sides of a split
  • Reconcile with version vectors, CRDTs, or last-writer-wins
  • Examples: Dynamo-style stores, Cassandra, Riak
  • Right for carts, feeds, caches, telemetry

PACELC: The Tradeoff When Nothing Is Broken

Daniel Abadi observed that CAP only describes behavior during partitions, yet partitions are rare. Most of the time the dominant tension is consistency versus latency: stronger consistency forces more replicas to coordinate, adding round trips. His PACELC formulation captures both axes:

if Partition → choose Availability or Consistency;
Else → choose Latency or Consistency

PACELC Decision Tree

Abadi's taxonomy maps real systems onto these combinations: Dynamo, Cassandra, and Riak are typically PA/EL; VoltDB/H-Store and Megastore are PC/EC; Yahoo's PNUTS is PC/EL; MongoDB is often described as roughly PA/EC. Labels are approximations — many databases expose tunable quorum levels that move them along the spectrum.

Real-World Use Cases

Payments & ledgers
Banks and payment rails lean CP/PC-EC: refusing a debit during a split beats double-spending. Reconciliation and compensating transactions clean up the rare overdraft (Brewer's ATM example).
Shopping carts
Amazon Dynamo famously chose availability: during a partition, carts on both sides accept updates and merge with a set-union CRDT-style approach, accepting that deleted items may reappear.
Global config / locks
Chubby, etcd, and ZooKeeper use consensus so only a quorum partition serves writes — classic CP behavior for coordination metadata.
CDN & edge caches
Content delivery networks are AP by nature: edge POPs keep serving slightly stale content rather than erroring when origin connectivity drops.
Design takeaway: do not treat CAP as a bumper sticker for your database. Decide per operation, per data type, and per failure mode. Detect partitions explicitly, limit operations that would break hard invariants, and plan recovery (including user-visible compensation) before you need it.

Closing Thought

CAP is an impossibility result, not a product feature matrix. It tells you that during a split you cannot have both linearizable reads and uninterrupted responses — full stop. Everything else is engineering: timeouts that define when you enter partition mode, quorum and consensus protocols when correctness matters more than uptime, eventual consistency and CRDTs when uptime matters more than a single global truth, and PACELC to remind you that even on a healthy network you still pay latency for stronger consistency. Master the precise definitions, then pick the tradeoffs your users can live with.
