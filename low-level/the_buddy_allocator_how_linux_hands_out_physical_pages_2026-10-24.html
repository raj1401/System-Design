The Buddy Allocator: How Linux Hands Out Physical Pages

Power-of-two blocks, one XOR to find your buddy, and decades of tuning to keep big contiguous chunks alive.

October 24, 2026 · 5 min read

Summary

Every physical page Linux uses, whether for a process's anonymous memory, the page cache, a slab, or a DMA buffer, ultimately comes from the page allocator, and at its core is a binary buddy allocator. Memory in each zone is tracked as free blocks of 2order contiguous pages. A request for an order-n block takes the smallest free block that fits and splits it in half repeatedly; each split produces two halves called buddies. When a block is freed, the allocator checks whether its buddy is also free and, if so, merges them into a block one order larger, repeating as far as it can. The idea dates back to Knowlton's 1965 "A Fast Storage Allocator" and is still the foundation of mm/page_alloc.c today.

Why power of two? Because a block of 2n pages is always aligned to 2n pages, the buddy of any block is found by flipping a single bit of its page frame number: buddy_pfn = pfn ^ (1 << order). No search, no tree walk, just one XOR.

Deeper dive

Zones, free areas and orders

Physical memory is split into NUMA nodes, and each node into zones such as ZONE_DMA32, ZONE_NORMAL and ZONE_MOVABLE. The kernel's physical memory documentation calls struct zone the core data structure of the page allocator. Each zone holds an array free_area[NR_PAGE_ORDERS] defined in include/linux/mmzone.h. Entry k holds every free block of exactly 2k pages, up to MAX_PAGE_ORDER (10 by default, so the largest block is 1024 pages, or 4 MiB with 4 KiB pages).

A free block is represented by the struct page of its first page. That head page is linked into the right list and records its order, so the allocator knows how big the block is without any separate metadata.

Allocation: split down

For an order-n request, the allocator looks at free_area[n]. If it is empty it tries n+1, n+2, and so on. When it finds a larger block of order m, it removes it, hands out one half, and puts the other half back on the m-1 list, repeating until it reaches order n. In the source this is __rmqueue_smallest() calling expand(). The cost is bounded by the number of orders, which is about eleven.

Free: coalesce up

Freeing goes through __free_one_page(). It computes the buddy's PFN with the XOR, checks that the buddy is free, is the same order, and is in the same zone, and if so unlinks it and merges the two into one block of order+1. The merged block's start is pfn & buddy_pfn, so the lower of the two addresses. The loop continues until the buddy is busy or the maximum order is reached. This eager coalescing is what lets the system rebuild large contiguous regions after a burst of small allocations.

Per-CPU pagesets: the fast path

Taking the zone lock for every single-page allocation would not scale on large machines. As the physical memory docs describe, the allocator first tries the Per-CPU Pagesets (PCP), which are small per-CPU caches of low-order pages, and only falls back to the buddy free areas when they run dry. Pages move between the PCP lists and the buddy lists in batches (rmqueue_bulk() to refill, free_pcppages_bulk() to drain), so the zone lock is amortized across many operations. The trade-off is that pages sitting on PCP lists can't coalesce, which is why high-order failures can trigger a PCP drain.

Migratetypes: fighting external fragmentation

A pure buddy system fragments badly: one long-lived unmovable kernel page in the middle of a 2 MiB region prevents that region from ever coalescing into a huge page. Mel Gorman's "grouping pages by mobility" patch set, merged for 2.6.24, split each free_area into per-migratetype lists. Memory is carved into pageblocks (typically the huge-page size) and each pageblock is tagged MIGRATE_UNMOVABLE, MIGRATE_MOVABLE or MIGRATE_RECLAIMABLE, with special types such as MIGRATE_HIGHATOMIC, MIGRATE_CMA and MIGRATE_ISOLATE. Allocations prefer their own type. When a type runs out, the allocator steals the largest block it can from another type and may claim the whole pageblock, so mixed usage stays clustered. That keeps movable pages together, where memory compaction can migrate them out of the way to rebuild contiguous ranges.

Watermarks and the slow path

Each zone has min, low and high watermarks derived from vm.min_free_kbytes. When free pages drop below low, kswapd is woken to reclaim in the background; below min, the allocating task may enter direct reclaim and direct compaction itself, as described in the zone documentation. GFP flags from Memory Allocation Controls decide which zones are eligible and whether the caller may sleep, reclaim or dip into reserves.

Strengths

  • O(orders) allocation and free, with no search
  • Buddy lookup is a single XOR on the PFN
  • Eager coalescing rebuilds large contiguous blocks
  • Natural alignment for huge pages and DMA

Weaknesses

  • Internal fragmentation: a 5-page need gets 8 pages
  • External fragmentation from pinned unmovable pages
  • Zone lock contention without PCP batching
  • Page-sized granularity, so small objects need slab on top

Real-world use cases

Huge pages
Transparent Huge Pages and hugetlbfs need order-9 (2 MiB on x86-64) blocks; mobility grouping and compaction exist largely to keep those available.
Slab caches
SLUB requests pages, sometimes higher-order ones, from the buddy allocator and carves them into objects for kmalloc.
Device DMA and CMA
Drivers needing physically contiguous buffers rely on high-order allocations or on the CMA migratetype, which lets movable pages borrow reserved regions.
Diagnosing fragmentation
/proc/buddyinfo shows free block counts per order per zone, and /proc/pagetypeinfo breaks them down by migratetype (see proc_buddyinfo(5)).
Still evolving. In 2026, Johannes Weiner and Rik van Riel posted RFC patches for a per-CPU pageblock buddy allocator that lets a CPU own whole pageblocks and merge buddies locally on its PCP, aiming to cut zone lock traffic without spreading fragmentation. It is an RFC, not merged behaviour.
