High-Level System Design

Raft: Distributed Consensus You Can Actually Reason About

How a cluster of unreliable machines agrees on one truth — one leader, one log, at a time.

August 3, 2026 · 5 min read

What Problem Is Raft Solving?

Any system that keeps more than one copy of data — a database, a lock service, a configuration store — eventually has to answer an uncomfortable question: what happens when two replicas disagree? Network delays, crashed machines, and partitions mean replicas will see events in different orders unless something forces them into agreement. That "something" is a consensus algorithm.

Consensus algorithms allow a collection of machines to work as a coherent group that can survive the failures of some of its members. For years the dominant answer was Paxos, but Paxos is quite difficult to understand and requires complex changes to support practical systems. In 2014, Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout published Raft with a very deliberate design goal: after struggling with Paxos themselves, they set out to find a new consensus algorithm that could provide a better foundation for system building and education — could they define a consensus algorithm for practical systems and describe it in a way that is significantly easier to learn than Paxos?

A user study with 43 students at two universities showed that Raft is significantly easier to understand than Paxos. Today more and more products, such as databases (etcd, ClickHouse) or messaging systems (RabbitMQ, Kafka), have incorporated it as their replication backbone.

Why this matters: Consensus is the invisible foundation under almost every "highly available" claim you've ever read in a systems README. If you understand Raft, you understand how etcd backs Kubernetes, how CockroachDB and TiKV replicate ranges, and how a "leader" is safely elected without two nodes ever believing they're both in charge at once.

The Core Idea: Replicated State Machines via a Replicated Log

Raft models the problem as a replicated state machine: every server runs the same deterministic state machine, and as long as they all execute the exact same sequence of commands, they end up in the exact same state. The consensus algorithm manages a replicated log containing state machine commands from clients, and the state machines process identical sequences of commands from the logs , so the servers stay in lockstep.

To enhance understandability, Raft separates the key elements of consensus — leader election, log replication, and safety — and it enforces a stronger degree of coherency to reduce the number of states that must be considered. Raft also includes a new mechanism for changing cluster membership, which uses overlapping majorities to guarantee safety. Two design choices make this tractable:

  • Strong leadership. Raft uses a stronger form of leadership than other consensus algorithms — log entries only flow from the leader to other servers, which simplifies the management of the replicated log.
  • Randomized timers. The leader election process starts with a randomized timer instead of a fixed timer, making conflict resolution easy.

Terms: Raft's Logical Clock

A term is an integer that represents a logical time in the Raft cluster life cycle. It lies at the heart of Raft, organizing leader elections and log replication — each log entry includes the term number alongside the operation, and the term increases monotonically after each election. Terms act as a tie-breaker: any message carrying a stale term is immediately rejected, which is what prevents an old, partitioned-away leader from corrupting the log when it reconnects.

The Three Roles

Nodes in the Raft algorithm have three roles: the Leader is responsible for handling client requests, managing log replication, and sending heartbeats; the Follower passively accepts logs and heartbeats from the Leader and does not initiate requests; and the Candidate is a Follower that, during the election process, transitions to initiate an election.

Leader Election

Every follower runs an election timer with a randomized duration. Leader elections are typically triggered when a follower does not receive a heartbeat from the leader within a certain period (the election timeout). When it fires, the follower becomes a candidate, increments its term, votes for itself, and sends RequestVote RPCs to every other node. If the candidate does not receive enough votes within the election timeout, the election fails, and the candidate waits for a random period before re-initiating the election to avoid split votes caused by multiple nodes starting elections simultaneously.

Randomization is doing real work here: because timeouts are jittered, one node almost always times out before the others, so it usually wins its election before a competing candidate even starts one. Safety is enforced during voting too — Raft ensures that only nodes with sufficiently up-to-date logs can become leaders by comparing the term and index of logs, which guarantees that the leader's log contains all committed entries. Modern implementations add a pre-vote phase: before formally initiating an election, a candidate can first send a Pre-Vote request to confirm whether it has a chance to win, avoiding unnecessary term increments. This stops a flaky, partitioned node from repeatedly bumping the term and destabilizing a healthy cluster.

Log Replication

Once elected, the leader handles client requests containing instructions to be executed by the state machines. The leader adds the instruction to its log as a new entry and sends AppendEntries RPCs to all other servers in parallel to replicate the entry. The same RPC doubles as a heartbeat: the leader is exclusively responsible for communication with clients, sending information to followers about new log entries, and it periodically sends heartbeats to maintain its leadership. An entry is considered committed — safe to apply to the state machine and return to the client — only once a majority of servers have durably stored it, which is exactly the majority-quorum guarantee that lets the cluster tolerate minority-side failures without losing data.

Diagram 2 — Log Replication via AppendEntries

Handling Inconsistent Logs

Followers can fall behind or hold stray uncommitted entries from a failed former leader. AppendEntries includes the index and term of the entry immediately preceding new ones; if a follower's log doesn't match, it rejects the request, and the leader decrements its stored "next index" for that follower and retries — walking backward until logs align, then overwriting any conflicting suffix. This log-matching property is what lets Raft repair divergence deterministically instead of merging conflicting histories.

Safety, Not Just Liveness

The clever part of Raft isn't the happy path — it's the invariants that hold even when nodes crash mid-replication or a network partition isolates the leader. Raft's Leader Completeness Property guarantees that any log entry committed in a given term will be present in the logs of all future leaders, because a candidate can only win an election if its log is at least as up-to-date as a majority of the cluster's logs — that's the same term/index comparison mentioned above. Combined with the commit rule (an entry counts only once a majority has it), this means a committed write can never be silently lost, even across repeated leader failures.

Why does this matter: These safety proofs are why Raft implementations bother with formal verification. Diego Ongaro's Ph.D. dissertation expands on the paper in much more detail and includes a formal specification of Raft written in TLA+. Independent teams have gone further, e.g. Woos, Wilcox, Anton, Tatlock, Ernst, and Anderson published "Planning for Change in a Formal Verification of the Raft Consensus Protocol" at CPP 2016.

Raft vs. Paxos vs. Viewstamped Replication

Raft

  • Single strong leader; log flows one direction
  • Understandability was an explicit design goal
  • Widely embedded: etcd, Consul, CockroachDB, TiKV
  • Well-defined membership-change protocol

Paxos / Multi-Paxos

  • Has two significant drawbacks — the first is that it is exceptionally difficult to understand
  • No single canonical leader-based description; many "flavors"
  • Powers classic systems like Chubby and Spanner-era infra
  • Harder to map directly onto an implementation

Raft is frequently compared to Viewstamped Replication as well — Raft is similar in many ways to existing consensus algorithms, most notably Oki and Liskov's Viewstamped Replication, but has several novel features like randomized timeouts and its specific log-matching safety argument.

Real-World Use Cases

Coordination store
etcd uses Raft for its replicated key-value store; etcd uses a leader-based consensus protocol for consistent data replication and log execution — cluster members elect a single leader, and the leader periodically sends heartbeats to maintain leadership. It underpins Kubernetes' cluster state.
Service mesh config
HashiCorp Consul uses Raft to replicate its service catalog and KV store across server agents, keeping service discovery data consistent during node failures.
Distributed SQL
CockroachDB and TiDB's TiKV layer run one Raft group per data shard/range, so each range independently elects a leader and replicates writes across nodes.
Messaging systems
Messaging systems such as RabbitMQ and Kafka have incorporated Raft (e.g. Kafka's KRaft mode) to replace ZooKeeper-based metadata coordination.

Operational Reality: Disk Latency Is Part of Liveness

A subtlety many teams learn the hard way: disk latency is part of leader liveness — if the cluster leader takes a minute to fsync a raft log update to disk, but the etcd cluster has a one-second election timeout, the leader is effectively unavailable because it can't commit any new proposals while waiting on the slow disk. This is why production Raft deployments care so much about fast, consistent-latency storage — the consensus algorithm is only as available as its slowest durable write.

Takeaways

Raft succeeded at its founding goal: it turned a notoriously opaque problem into something a small team can implement, test, and reason about with confidence. By separating leader election, log replication, and safety into distinct, well-specified mechanisms — terms as a logical clock, randomized election timeouts, majority-quorum commits, and log-matching repair — it gives system designers a consensus primitive that's simple enough to audit and robust enough to run some of the world's largest coordination layers.
